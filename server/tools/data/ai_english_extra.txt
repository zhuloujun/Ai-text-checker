In the Lin'an district there lived an old man surnamed Ge who made his living selling flatbread outside the Houchao Gate. Every day he rose at the fifth watch to knead the dough and light the stove, and he kept to this routine for decades without fail. One night, during a heavy snowfall, a young man knocked on his door asking for bread. His clothes were thin, yet his features were refined and his speech gentle and clear. Taking pity on him in the cold, the old man invited him to sit by the stove and gave him two warm cakes, refusing any payment. When the young man had eaten, he bowed his thanks and departed. After only a few steps he vanished, leaving not a single footprint in the snow. The following spring, the old man fell gravely ill during an epidemic. In a dream, the young man appeared, laid a hand upon his head, and said, "For the kindness of a single cake, I now repay you." The old man awoke drenched in sweat, and his illness was cured.

Zhou Wenhan, courtesy name Boyuan, was a native of Xi'an in Quzhou. Orphaned at a young age, he was taught to read by his mother; by day he gathered firewood, and by night he recited his lessons without pause. During the Shaoxing era he passed the metropolitan examination and was appointed revenue administrator of Xinzhou. At that time the prefect was greedy and cruel, and many of the common people suffered unjust imprisonment. Wenhan repeatedly contested these cases according to the law, and the prefect was unable to overrule him. When his term ended, he was reassigned as magistrate of Longquan County. The county was mountainous and plagued by drought each year, so Wenhan surveyed the terrain and channeled the streams into irrigation canals, watering several thousand qing of fields. The people relied upon these works and called them "Lord Zhou's Canals." He died in office at the age of fifty-eight, and the scholars and commoners wept for him.

Within the garden lay a pond, and along its banks stood several ancient willows. During the Tianqi reign, a certain student was studying there when, one night, he heard laughter and conversation drifting from the pond. Stepping outside, he saw several young women washing silk beneath the moon. Their faces were lovely, and their skirts and robes were all fashioned in the style of a former dynasty. The student approached and bowed to them, but the women merely exchanged glances, smiled, and slipped together into the water, leaving only a few lengths of silk floating away on the ripples. Wang Zhi was a woodcutter of Jinhua. One day he went into the mountains to cut timber and came upon two boys playing a game of weiqi. Setting down his axe, he stood to watch them. The boys gave him something shaped like a date pit, and when he held it in his mouth he felt no hunger. When the game ended, one of the boys said to him, "Why do you not go home?" Wang Zhi rose to pick up his axe, only to find that its wooden handle had completely rotted away. When he returned to his village, he discovered that several decades had passed, and none of his relatives or old acquaintances remained alive. This tale has been widely told through the ages, and the mountain has since been known as Lanke Mountain, the Mountain of the Rotten Axe Handle.

The fiscal administration of the Southern Song represents a crucial yet often overlooked dimension of the dynasty's political history. Following the loss of the north in 1127, the court faced the formidable challenge of sustaining a large standing army while governing a significantly reduced territorial base. Scholars have traditionally interpreted the proliferation of surtaxes, such as the jingzhizhi qian, as evidence of administrative decay and fiscal predation. However, a closer examination of local gazetteers and official memorials suggests a more nuanced picture. These levies were not merely extractive devices but also reflected a pragmatic adaptation to the pressures of frontier defense. By situating fiscal policy within the broader context of military necessity, this study seeks to shed light on the complex interplay between central authority and local society in twelfth-century China.

The establishment of the branch secretariats under the Yuan dynasty marked a pivotal transformation in the administrative geography of China. Originally conceived as temporary extensions of central authority during military campaigns, these institutions gradually evolved into permanent organs of provincial governance. This article argues that the Yuan system of branch secretariats laid the foundation for the provincial structure that would endure through the Ming and Qing dynasties and, in modified form, into the modern era. Drawing on the Yuanshi, the Yuan dianzhang, and a range of epigraphic sources, it traces the institutional development of these bodies from the reign of Ögödei to the late Zhizheng period. Particular attention is paid to the tensions between Mongol political traditions and inherited Chinese bureaucratic practices.

The Grand Secretariat of the Ming dynasty occupies a paradoxical position in the history of Chinese political institutions. Formally, the grand secretaries held relatively low official ranks and served merely as advisers to the emperor following the abolition of the Chancellery in 1380. In practice, however, their control over the drafting of rescripts allowed them to exercise considerable influence over the formulation of imperial policy. This influence was further complicated by the role of the Directorate of Ceremonial, whose eunuchs held the authority to affix the vermilion endorsement. Understanding this dual structure is essential for making sense of the recurrent conflicts between civil officials and palace eunuchs that characterized much of the dynasty's political life. The present study examines these dynamics through a close reading of memorials from the Jiajing and Wanli reigns.

Local academies played an increasingly important role in the intellectual and social life of Song China. While the four great academies of the Northern Song are well documented, far less attention has been paid to the hundreds of smaller institutions that flourished in the prefectures of the south. It suggests that the proliferation of academies should be understood as part of a broader localist turn in Southern Song elite strategies.

Historians have long debated the extent to which the Mongol conquest disrupted the economic foundations of Jiangnan society. Earlier scholarship tended to emphasize the destructive impact of warfare and the heavy exactions imposed by the new regime. More recent studies, however, have highlighted the remarkable resilience of the regional economy and the continuity of commercial networks across the Song-Yuan transition. This essay builds on this revisionist literature by examining land contracts and family registers from the Huizhou region. The evidence indicates that while the conquest undoubtedly caused significant short-term dislocation, the underlying patterns of landholding and commercial activity remained largely intact. Moreover, the integration of China into a larger Eurasian empire opened new opportunities for long-distance trade that some local families were quick to exploit.

# academic
The compilation of the Song History in the final years of the Yuan dynasty was a monumental undertaking completed in remarkably little time. Critics since the Ming have pointed to its numerous inconsistencies, redundancies, and factual errors as evidence of hasty and careless editorial work. Yet such criticisms, while not unfounded, risk obscuring the text's considerable value as a repository of materials that would otherwise have been lost. Many of the biographies, treatises, and tables in the Song History were derived directly from state historiographical records, including veritable records and national histories compiled during the Song itself. Consequently, the text preserves a wealth of detail concerning official careers, institutional arrangements, and fiscal statistics. Historians must therefore approach it with both critical caution and genuine appreciation.

The phenomenon of fabricated loyalist narratives in local historiography deserves far greater scholarly attention than it has hitherto received. In many regions of southern China, gazetteers compiled during the Ming and Qing periods celebrate local heroes who allegedly resisted the Mongol invasion at the end of the Song. Upon closer inspection, however, a significant number of these figures cannot be verified in any contemporary source. Their biographies frequently appear for the first time centuries after the events they describe, and they often exhibit striking similarities in narrative structure and moral emphasis. This article proposes a methodological framework for identifying such fabricated narratives, combining textual genealogy, cross-referencing with Yuan-era sources, and attention to the social contexts in which these stories emerged and were promoted by local elites.

Examination culture in late imperial China was not merely a mechanism for bureaucratic recruitment; it was a pervasive social institution that shaped family strategies, educational practices, and literary production across the empire. The eight-legged essay, often dismissed by modern critics as a rigid and formulaic genre, in fact demanded considerable rhetorical skill and a thorough command of the classical canon. Candidates devoted years of intensive study to mastering its conventions, and the resulting literary habits left a lasting imprint on the prose of the educated elite. This chapter examines the pedagogical materials used to train examination candidates in the Ming, including model essays, commentaries, and handbooks, in order to reconstruct the intellectual world of those who aspired to success in the examinations.

Water control has long been recognized as a central concern of the Chinese state, yet the specific mechanisms through which hydraulic projects were planned, financed, and maintained at the local level remain insufficiently understood. This study focuses on the polders of the Lake Tai basin during the Song and Yuan periods. Using a combination of official documents, private writings, and archaeological evidence, it reconstructs the processes through which polders were constructed and the conflicts that arose over their management. The findings reveal a dynamic interplay between state initiatives and local agency. Officials frequently relied upon the cooperation of powerful landholding families, whose interests did not always align with those of smallholders downstream. Ultimately, the history of the polders illuminates the broader tensions inherent in the governance of a densely populated agrarian society.

Religious life in Song China was characterized by a remarkable diversity of practices and institutions. Buddhist monasteries, Daoist temples, and local cults coexisted and competed for patronage, while the state sought to regulate religious activity through a system of official recognition and titles. The granting of plaques and honorific titles to local deities, in particular, provided a means by which the court could incorporate popular cults into the orthodox religious order. This paper examines the process through which local communities petitioned for such recognition, analyzing the documents submitted to the Ministry of Rites and the criteria by which petitions were evaluated. It argues that the system of official recognition functioned not merely as an instrument of state control but also as a channel for negotiation between the court and local society.

# academic
The transition from the Yuan to the Ming dynasty has often been portrayed as a restoration of native Chinese rule following nearly a century of Mongol domination. This narrative, promoted vigorously by the Ming founder himself, has exerted a powerful influence on subsequent historiography. Nevertheless, it obscures the many continuities that linked the two regimes. The early Ming state inherited a considerable portion of its administrative apparatus, military organization, and legal framework from its predecessor. Moreover, many of the officials who served the new dynasty had begun their careers under the Yuan. By emphasizing these continuities, this essay seeks to move beyond the rhetoric of restoration and to offer a more balanced assessment of the institutional legacy of Mongol rule in China.
