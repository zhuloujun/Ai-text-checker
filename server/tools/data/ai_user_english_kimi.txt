Every afternoon at four, Mr. Alden carried a tin of sardines to the park and fed a tabby cat he pretended to despise. "Too proud to eat off the ground," he'd grumble. "Your mother was the same."
He had buried his wife, Marion, in the spring. The cat had appeared in June, thin and loud, wearing a collar too worn to read.
One November day the cat did not come. He waited until the lamps came on, and something in his chest waited with him. The next morning she returned, collar gone, and left in his lap a small brass tag. He did not need the jeweler to read it. He had written it himself, forty-three years ago, on their first anniversary:
"To M. — Eat anyway. — A."
That winter he stopped telling people he disliked cats. When asked, he said only, "We have an arrangement. She watches the weather. I keep dinner on time." And his neighbors noticed that for the rest of his life, Mr. Alden was never, ever late.

When my uncle died, he left me the house on Brier Lane and one instruction: never open the north parlor wall.
The house ticks. Not the plumbing—his letter admitted as much. "You will hear it," he wrote, "at the hour you least expect to be listened to."
For months I mocked the old man's madness. Then, on the night I resolved to sell the place, the ticking began inside my own bedroom wall—keeping time with, no, half a beat behind, my heart.
I took up the crowbar.
Behind the lath I found no corpse, no treasure: only a brass mantel clock, stopped, wrapped in my christening gown, and a note in my uncle's hand: "It was given to me the day you were born, and it stopped the night your parents' carriage went into the river. I have spent forty years deciding whether to wind it. I leave the decision to you. A stopped clock is honest; a wound one makes promises."
The ticking ceased. I have not wound it. But some nights I wake, and the house is holding its breath, waiting to see whether I have learned my uncle's arithmetic: that some griefs are kept wound, and some are allowed to stop.

Once a month, when the moon was thin and busy, she let down a silver ladder into the dreams of children, so they might climb a little way and return with something shining: a tune, a courage, a word they would need years later.
But Tomas the umbrella-maker had never once dreamed. He repaired other people's weather, he said, and had none of his own.
One autumn morning he found a splinter of moonlight caught in his awning, cool as a fishbone, bright as a promise. He wove it into the cloth of an umbrella, and when he opened it over the drought-stricken village, it rained—softly, politely, exactly enough.
Every village after that wanted a moon-umbrella. Tomas wove the splinter thinner and thinner, and the moon, who is not unkind but is very forgetful, forgot to remember his name.
By winter the splinter was gone, and Tomas could make only ordinary umbrellas again. The villagers pitied him. But whenever it rained on a wedding, or a funeral, or a field that had given up, someone would look up and swear the drops were silver at the edges.
"It is nothing," Tomas would say. "A little moon left in the seams."
For it is well known among umbrellas that the moon forgets every face she borrows light from—and that every raindrop remembers the sky.

The supply steamer came on the first Tuesday of every month, and with it came Mr. Pruitt, the inspector's clerk, carrying his leather case of forms.
"One lamp. One barometer. Oil, four gallons. Biscuits, ten pounds." Pruitt read the list without looking up. "Sign here."
Lukash, the keeper of Skerry Head light, signed. He had signed every month for twenty-six years.
One October the oil did not come. "Shortage," said Pruitt. "The form, however, is not short. Sign here."
"How can I sign for oil that isn't?"
"If you do not sign," said Pruitt pleasantly, "the form shows a deficit, and a deficit means an inquiry, and an inquiry means, very likely, a new keeper."
Lukash signed. He burned the spare lamp wicks that winter and fished by daylight, and the light never failed.
Years later, retired to the mainland, he met Pruitt at the pier, old now, pensioned, carrying his own parcels.
"You remember me? Skerry Head. The oil."
Pruitt considered. "I remember the form," he said at last, and it was not clear whether he was proud or sorry.
At home Lukash's grandson found a yellow paper framed above the desk and asked what it was.
"A receipt," said the old man.
"For what?"
Lukash looked out at the sea, which was the color of pewter and entirely without forms.
"For thirty years of oil that I never received," he said. He laughed. "It is the only letter the government ever wrote me."

The tailor's shop was on a narrow street near the harbor. The sign said ALTERATIONS. The light inside was good.
The man brought the coat in a cloth bag. "It's my father's," he said. "I want it to fit me."
The tailor spread the coat on the cutting table. It was old, well made, English cloth. "It was made for a bigger man," he said.
"Yes."
"And a man who stood straighter."
"He did," the customer said. "Toward the end he didn't."
The tailor measured. "Come back Thursday."
On Thursday the coat was on its hanger, the seams opened, the sleeves chalked. The man tried it. The shoulders were still a little wide.
"My father always said a coat should outlive you," he said. "That's why he bought good cloth."
"A coat is not a man," said the tailor.
"No. But you keep it."
The man paid and put the coat on. Outside, it had begun to rain. He stood at the corner where the bus stopped. The tailor watched from the doorway.
The man did not open his umbrella. He turned up the collar of his father's coat and walked home through the rain, and the coat fitted well enough.

The chapel of St. Nobody stood at the end of Crooked Lane, which is to say at the end of nothing at all, for the lane had been unmade thirty years before the railway came, and the railway, having come, declined to stop there. The chapel had a tower, and in the tower a bell, and the bell had not been rung in forty years, because the chapel had no parish, and the parish, such as it was, had no bell-ringer, and the one man who remembered the rope was Mr. Grubb, the sexton, who said the rope was damp.
Mr. Grubb kept the key in his pocket and the cold in his heart. He had buried three wives and outlived his only son, and he charged the village a shilling for every funeral and sixpence for advice, which he gave freely and often.
On Christmas Eve, when the frost made stars of the cobbles, an old woman knocked at his door. She wore a shawl the color of an old penny.
"I should like to ring the bell," she said, "for the dead."
"The bell is damp," said Mr. Grubb.
"Then I shall ring it with my hands," she said, and showed him her hands, which were small and red and like a child's.
"For whom?" said Mr. Grubb, because business was business.
"For them as has no one," she said.
Now it happened—though Mr. Grubb never told it this way, for he was an honest man except in the matter of ropes—it happened that in the night he woke and heard the bell. Not rung; only spoken to, gently, the way one touches a shoulder. And he remembered being seven years old, lost in the fog on the marshes, and following, through the dark, the sound of that same bell to his mother's door.
He rose. He put on his greatcoat and his conscience, and in the morning—for bells ring slowly in the dark—he opened the chapel, and set the fire, and paid the old woman her shilling, though she had rung nothing at all, or everything, which comes to the same.
The villagers came, first for the warmth, then for the singing, which was the old woman's, and stayed for the pudding, which was Mrs. Trivett's. And Mr. Grubb, who had not sung since his son's funeral, sang "The First Nowell" in a voice like an old door opening, and found it opened inward.
They say the chapel of St. Nobody has a parish now, though it is a parish without boundaries, and that its bell rings only on Christmas Eve, and only for those who have no one. And they say that if you ask the sexton why he lets a damp bell ring, he puts his hand in his pocket, where the key is, and answers, as he learned from an old woman with small red hands:
"For them as has no one. And for them as did not know they had."
